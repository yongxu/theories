## 3. MUST, then LESSER

**MUST**

- **The first false proof step is the last sentence of the sketch of (b).** The doubling induction is valid. The definition of `D` says which *parents* are unmet. It puts no restriction on children of met outsiders. This also fails after an actual level crossing. Change the counterexample above to `need T=10, N=6, C=10`. Output `TTK@10 → TTTTTK@9 → TK@5 → TK@5; D=1, E=absent, R=absent`. The design attaches outside closure only to “ever after”. It is needed for the general claim about the very next way as well. Of 11,349 eligible always-`FFirst` course cases in my sweep, 3,961 violated that claim. The designer’s `centre.py` lines 278–296 check a deadline and survival of a suffix. Neither predicate checks for a favoured child at `D+1`.
- **Exclusion does not identify crowding.** Recounting the designer’s 211,248 `general2` courses reproduced the 101,384 labelled “with the slots filled”. Only 77,600 filled the slots at `E`. Only 29,520 both filled them and left an available outsider unformed. Of 74,504 labelled “the rest unmet first”, 17,304 actually had a met outsider at `E`. A met outsider can have no kids. Even an always-fill policy can reach `E` with space left. For example, with four slots, needs `(1,8)`, uses `(1,1)`, and kids `(TT,K)`, output from `TK@7` is `TT@5; E=0`. The last sentence of (b) and the §6 outcome labels give `E` a cause its definition cannot detect.
- **The alarm sweep did not measure the stated `R`.** Line 361 uses the first level below the alarm. In T4 from level 19 with alarm 20, the alarm fires at step zero, all four candidates fit, and the implemented choice still satisfies `FFirst`. Output `D=7, E=1, R=absent, end=7`. Substituting the earlier alarm can make the deadline easier to pass.
- **Kind priority and registered information are different claims.** In T4 the yield list already identifies every giver. Choosing the *lower* yield from that same observation gives `TKK@20 → TTKK@20 → TTKK@20`, the surviving course. In the wider alarm sweep some systems have equal yields for both kinds and zero giving for both. On candidate lists `TTTTKK` and `KKTTTT`, both observations agree position by position. To take four `T`s requires positions `[0,1,2,3]` and `[2,3,4,5]` respectively. The code can do that by reading kind. Its full rescue counts cannot be attributed to the named readings. T4 establishes the effect of a choice rule.
- **The start bound matters to (c).** Section 1 supplies `length w₀ ≤ N`, but §2 uses the full product type and (c)’s premise list omits the bound. Read literally on that product, take `N=2, C=a=8, b=c=d=1, k=2`. From `TKKK@6` the printed formula says “ends”. Output `TKKK@6 → KK@4 → KK@4`. The proof at `D=0` charges the later pure-`K` way for three keepers although only two fit. This counterexample uses precisely the extra starts admitted by the product.
- **T5’s stated corner changes the type.** Every §2 system has natural-number slots and cap, and a selector that fits on *every* input list. No finite slot count admits `pick c x = c` on every list. Arbitrary external giving is also outside the stated base update. The old stock step can be compared in the proposed extension. The claimed unchanged instantiation of `LineOnAStock.ways_alike` does not follow by choosing those parameters in §2. The displayed corner traces themselves reproduced. Forming all candidates repeats `CBCB@8` from 6 and ends at step 5 from 7. A sublist choice from 7 repeats `CB@12`.

**LESSER**

- Section 1’s ceiling logarithm and §2’s zero-based deadline differ. At `N=4, n₀=1`, they give 2 and 1 respectively. T4 attains the latter. Also, `E+1` is guaranteed to be unkeepable, but need not be the *first* unkeepable state. With `N=5, C=17, a=b=c=d=1, k=2`, output is `TK@3 → TTK@2 → TTTTK@0 → ∅@0`, free keepability `true,false,false,false`, and `E=2`.
- The deadline decides the order of events for an always-`FFirst` course. Survival after `D` can still depend on a later choice. With `N=2, C=3, T=(3,1,0,TT), K=(1,0,0,K)`, two such selectors share `TK@2 → K@1` through the deadline. One repeats `K@1`. The other next chooses empty and ends.
- “The menu only narrows” needs the reachability qualification in Lemma 2. Immediate kinds can increase when one kind leaves kids of two kinds. Also, a picked list alone does not specify picked *positions* when a candidate occurs twice. The reading claim needs an embedding or a convention for ties.
- In (c), the pure-`K` deficit below `2dg` **doubles** before truncation. For `d=3,g=1,b=1`, output levels are `5,4,2,0`. Also the printed `x′` uses `d g_D` even if `x_D<b`. It represents the next level only when that giving is available or clipping masks the difference. At `N=3,C=a=12,b=6,c=d=1`, `TK@5` gives actual next level 3 and printed value 4. The separate `b≤x_D` conjunct keeps the survival verdict correct.

## 4. Does a general law of the design do work that the old laws do not?

**Yes, within this model.** While all favoured parents are met and priority is respected, the next favoured count is at least `min(N,2n)`. This forces a deadline from the initial count and the slots. At an actual exclusion, descent closure and stock expenditure then rule out *every* later choice, even at the cap. In the two-kind family the expenditure between doublings also gives a numerical prediction before running the course. The old statements I read do different work. `GuardHorizon.closed_viable` starts from an already closed region, `LineOnAStock.ways_alike` transports equal ways across equal need tests, and `RuleCapture.turn_locked` propagates an already locked rule under its share hypotheses. A derivation of old results on both sides using G1’s deadline or price law was not found in what I read. G1 also gives no law of a widening future.

## 5. The specific questions

**(a) Beyond the designer’s box.** I used three favoured kinds with respective kids `BA, CAB, CB`, all with one common need, and a fourth kind whose kids varied by system. The four larger systems had `(N,C)=(5,13),(5,15),(7,21),(7,24)`. Two allowed an outside parent to have a favoured child. I included zero-slot and zero-cap corners, all legal starting multiplicities and levels, and seven selectors, including one that may leave slots empty. Output was `courses 135072, (a) failures 0, deadline failures 0, (b)(II) failures 3961, L1 failures 0, L2 failures 0`. Part (c) fixes two kinds by definition. I enlarged its slots to 12, multiplier to 4, and cap to 188.

**(b) Try to break Lemma 1.** At the same way, a higher level preserves each old candidate in order. One can replay the same sublist. The residual and the giving from met units both weakly increase, so the resulting level is also at least as high. This gives the horizon induction. With an added term `γ(z)` at residual `z`, nondecreasing `z+γ(z)` suffices. In a two-kind system I tried all 1,024 schedules with five values in `0..3`. Of 364 with nondecreasing total, zero lost a viable horizon. Of 660 with a falling total somewhere, 192 lost a finite-horizon comparison and 160 lost infinite keepability. A decrease in giving alone need not hurt. For `γ(z)=max(4−z,0)`, the total is nondecreasing.

A permanent reversal needs just one kind that leaves itself, with need 4, use 2, one slot and cap 5. With zero internal giving and `γ(z)=2` for `z≤2`, zero otherwise, output is `A@4 → A@4` and `A@5 → A@3`, where the need fails. The only alternative move is empty. With no external term and the met unit instead giving 2 at levels at most 4 and zero above, the same two outputs result. The least kept level no longer defines an upper interval in either variant.

**(c) The price and its true condition.** Write `p=cn` and `h=dg` at a mixed step where both kinds are met. The update is `l=max(0,x−p−h)`, `y=l+min(l,h)`. The cap cannot bind above this value because `y≤x`. For the **exact geometric price in ordinary arithmetic**, `y=x−p` precisely when **`x≥p+2h` or `x=p`**. The latter equality is a complete drawdown to zero. There were zero discrepancies in 25,920 direct comparisons, including 324 cases of that isolated equality with positive `h`.

A sharp uniform lower bound on `a` for that price on every mixed step *used before saturation* is

`B = max { cn + 2d(N−n) | 1 ≤ n ≤ floor((N−1)/k) }`, with empty maximum zero.

Indeed, `kn<N` on those steps and at most `N−n` keepers fit. Conversely one can start with the maximizing counts. With a sufficiently high cap, a level one below that maximum breaks the ordinary-arithmetic price whenever the bound can be lowered to a legal positive need. This is a uniform bound for the price. Individual survival predictions can agree even after the price fails.

| Region in the enlarged search | Starts | Wrong printed branch, state at D, or fate | Wrong fate alone |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| `a ≥ 2(c+d)N` | 2,017,656 | 0 | 0 |
| `B ≤ a < 2(c+d)N` | 1,414,249 | 0 | 0 |
| `a < B` | 423,986 | 2,607 | 8 |

One unit below the sharper bound already changes fate. At `N=5,k=3,c=d=b=1,a=8,B=9`, the formula predicts survival from `TKKKK@8`. Output `TKKKK@8 → TTTKK@6 → KK@2 → KK@0`. The designer’s nearest miss goes both ways. At `N=3,k=2,c=1,d=2,a=7,b=1`, from `TKK@7` the formula says survive and `K@0` ends. From `TKK@8` it says end and `K@4` repeats.

There is an exact **survival** condition even when the price fails. For each putative first unmet step `i=0,…,t*`, take the pre-saturation counts `n_j=n₀k^j`, `g_j=min(g₀,N−n_j)`. A turn at `i` survives exactly if its actual level is in `[c n_i + max(b,2d g_i), a)` and all earlier favoured needs were met. To decide this from the start, pull each endpoint `q` back through `j=i−1,…,0`, replacing it by

`max(a, c n_j + max(q, d g_j + ceil(q/2)))`.

The start survives exactly when `x₀` belongs to at least one resulting half-open interval. This follows because for every positive target `q`, the mixed update reaches `y≥q` exactly when `x≥p+max(q,h+ceil(q/2))`. At the turn the remaining keepers require next level at least `max(b,2h)`. The inverse had zero mismatches in 414,720 one-step comparisons, and the interval criterion had zero fate mismatches across all 3,855,891 enlarged starts.

**(d) Full cap and every removal on the same T4 start.** With `T=(4,1,0,TT), K=(2,1,5,K), N=4, C=20`, yield priority gives `TKK@20 → TTKK@20 → TTTT@20`. Free keepability along those states is `true,true,false`. Thus the pre-choice state at `E=1` is still keepable. The choice produces the unkeepable state at `E+1=2`. Giving priority repeats `TTKK@20`. The same cap construction works from a full cap of any size `C≥8`, since both mixed updates can replenish their entire spend.

These are fixed positional tie conventions, each usable before and after removal. Thus **one fully specified T4 pair does depend on all four parts**, using rightmost ties. With leftmost ties the cost removal leaves the fate difference intact. Removing slots makes both choose the same growing population and overdraw it. Removing giving can leave the two population histories different even though both end.

I also reproduced the separate T3 instance I2. Its yield course ends at 2. Removing slots leaves a repeating `K@8`, removing multiplication leaves `K@12`, and changing to giving priority leaves `K@9`. Removing the need and use lets `TTTT@17` repeat. With giving removed, zero configurations were freely keepable. With need 9 instead of 14 and the same level-15 start, removing slots ends at 4. I3, which drops H5, repeats its initial three-unit way at level 4 with all three events absent.

**(e) Reset the favouring from candidate share.** Retain the current favoured group if it supplies at least two thirds of the candidates, otherwise switch. Start favouring `T`. Use five slots, cap 16, all needs 1, `T→TT` with use 1 and give 0, and outsiders `P→Q, Q→HHHH, H→H`, each with use 0 and give 6. Output from `TP@16` is `TTQ@16 → THHHH@16 → THHHH@16`. The incumbent shares are `2/3, 4/8, 4/6`, giving priorities `T, outside, outside`. Against the original fixed set `F={T}`, output is `D=absent, E=absent, R=1, t̂=2`. Fixed `T` priority on the identical start instead gives `E=2, end=7`. The three-event deadline survives even a history-dependent update because its proof only needs actual `FFirst` choices up to the first violation. The two-event conclusion cannot be carried over after a change in priority. The independent defect in (b)(II) remains.
